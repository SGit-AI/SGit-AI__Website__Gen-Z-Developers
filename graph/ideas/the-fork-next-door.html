Open source means nobody can lock you in: the fork next door

Anyone can fork, which protects founders, customers and investors from each other; forks take hold only when someone stops executing; six misconceptions; open source is about to become the most secure software.

Decided by. Nobody yet: a candidate for Dinis to read, comment on and decide (keep, rewrite, merge, cut, article first, later, not doing it).

Note. The fork as the investor's insurance in place of control: 'A competitor who forks our code today gets our position as of today. They do not get our velocity.' Two conditions make it real: a genuinely open licence, not open core, and a copyright structure not gathered in one corporate holder (every catalogued relicensing between 2018 and 2024 was by a single corporate copyright holder). The fork disciplines the founder because it is possible and protects the founder because it is slow. Relicensing has a pattern: HashiCorp, Elastic and Redis each moved from an open licence to a source-available one, each produced a well-funded fork backed by the cloud providers it targeted (OpenTofu, OpenSearch, Valkey), and two of the three have since added an open licence back. Docker Desktop's paid tier for larger companies (August 2021) worked commercially, with annual recurring revenue past $50 million within months, and the alternatives grew: OrbStack, Colima, Podman Desktop and Rancher Desktop together are about 47% of the five tools' installs through Homebrew over the past year, a figure the article says overstates them. The buyer's side of the same protection: if the only people who can fix a tool are one partner's engineers, the company has swapped a deployment gap for a dependency. With the code open and the record kept, 'who will fix it?' has many answers: your team, the partner, another partner, or the agents, under a mandate, reading the record. Dinis declares a stake: his companies sell open source.
