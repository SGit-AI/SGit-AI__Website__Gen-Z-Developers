Civic responsibility: to hack means to solve problems, and small countries can lead now

Dinis's Portugal talks as the then-and-now: what regulation delivered, the Portuguese Hacking Service, judo for small countries, sovereignty as the ability to move.

Decided by. Nobody yet: a candidate for Dinis to read, comment on and decide (keep, rewrite, merge, cut, article first, later, not doing it).

Note. Portuguese edition or both languages is Dinis's open question. Sovereignty as the ability to move, in its practical form: what stops workloads moving is code, not policy or price, and a twin that the application cannot tell from the real service makes the destination a setting. AWS's European Sovereign Cloud went live on 15 January 2026 (first region in Brandenburg, operated by EU residents, 'zero operational control outside of EU borders'), and the article's reading is that sovereignty which depends on one company's API is sovereignty by permission. There is no sovereignty without open source and open schemas, because sovereignty means you can leave; the judo move for an operator in Romania, Portugal or the UK is to use the customer's existing code, the hyperscaler's own API and open source as the weight rather than fight them. The word is facilitate, an embassy's job, and the SGraph Send Ambassador role was written to work the way an embassy works: represent, promote trade, build relationships, never hard sales, 'to create the conditions for that organic growth'. The judo playbook in five moves: map the attrition, commoditise the plumbing, create the demand, put the money where the market is still genesis, regulate what settled, then map again, each move using the weight of the larger players rather than matching it. Europe's advantage is strongest in its smaller countries, which can change faster. The article says it is not a national strategy but a map and a set of moves from one person's experience.
