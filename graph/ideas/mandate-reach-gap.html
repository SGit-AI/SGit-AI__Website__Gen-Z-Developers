Mandate, reach and the gap: hope versus enforcement

Before you give an agent access, write down what it is for and what it can reach; the gap is the risk; rules in a prompt are hope, rules in a hook or platform are enforcement; behaviour, decision and workflow policies are different things.

Decided by. Nobody yet: a candidate for Dinis to read, comment on and decide (keep, rewrite, merge, cut, article first, later, not doing it).

Note. The product in three words: a hive, a memory and a mandate. Ruflo gives the many hands, an sgit vault the memory the client keeps, an Agent Behaviour Policy each role's mandate. In the first proof of concept the same eleven actions went through the policy engine in enforce mode and six were stopped: four denied, two sent to a person. Read through the Agent Behaviour Policy, a desktop is the agent's grant (everything it can reach) and the task is its mandate; the desktop's job is to keep the first close to the second and leave a footprint that can be read afterwards. That gives nine properties, and the design that follows writes one mandate per task before the desktop exists (what it is for, the hosts it may reach, the secrets it may use and for which hosts, the identity, how long), configures the desktop from it so the grant cannot drift past it by accident, and keeps a vault outside every desktop that it can append to and cannot rewrite. The four objects as the article tables them: mandate (elicited from business and user stories, locked under change control), grant or reach (measured, calibrated against logs), delta (reach minus mandate, derived and never authored) and barriers (none, expectation, setting, boundary; only a boundary is a control). The goal of every engagement is the smallest mandate, the smallest reach, the smallest gap. Self-policing is the fallback, not the goal: the value is in the act of creating the policy, which discovers what is actually possible. Reality is the calibrator: the documentation said an agent could not attach a file to an email, and Claude found a way. Anthropic's own words for why a rule in a prompt is hope: CLAUDE.md files are treated 'as context, not enforced configuration. To block an action regardless of what Claude decides, use a PreToolUse hook instead', and Claude Code's permission rules 'are enforced by Claude Code, not by the model'. Both modes of reaching a model (the API, where we write the whole system prompt, and the apps and Claude Code, where the vendor's prompt comes first) carry the policy as text the model is asked to keep. Hope or enforcement measured the share of a policy resting on hope falling from 97% to 23% with the right design. The one-sentence test for the barrier column: 'A control bounds a grant only if it is enforced by something the grant does not include.' Atlassian's Rovo walked through as the shape of enterprise oversharing: nobody's permissions changed, what changed is that everything they already allowed is now one question away, and who the agent runs as (the person asking, the automation's connection account, or the agent's own account) decides the grant. The Northstar refund as why a behaviour policy is not enough: allowed to refund up to $200, owed $120, paid $150, and every control passes; a decision policy and a workflow policy answer 'who decides' and 'in what order'. The Northstar figures are a scripted simulation, no model called.
