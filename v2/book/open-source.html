Open source

In 2018 I asked a question I already knew the answer to: "Why don't you open source your code?" The answers, I wrote, would be some mix of "I don't think my code is good enough", "I'm embarrassed about my code" and "Nobody will want to use my code". My advice was blunt: "The key is to just do it!" Create a repository, add a licence file, and that is it. Then I told the truth about what happens next: "Nothing!" Most open source is maintained by a very small number of people.

Eight years on, everything in that chapter still holds, and the reason to do it has become clearer to me. It was never about contributions. It is that nobody can lock you in.

What open source is, and what it is not

Open source is a copyright licence. Copyright gives the person who creates a work the right to control copying; an open source licence is the holder using exactly that right to grant the opposite: you may copy, use, change and share, on these terms. The GNU General Public License, in 1989, made sharing a condition: build on it and distribute the result, and the result carries the same licence. Critics called that viral. The term "open source" came in 1998, after Netscape decided to release its browser's source, as a pragmatic reframing of the same freedoms for businesses. Linux, the most pragmatic project of all, stayed on the GPL. The repositories behind the newsroom are Apache 2.0 for code and Creative Commons for writing, and this book is CC BY 4.0, as the first edition was.

One thing I believed for a while, and was wrong about, because I hear it often from people your age: I thought the most open thing was not to licence at all. It is the opposite. With no licence, ordinary copyright applies and all rights are reserved. If you want people to use your work, you have to say so, and a licence is how you say it.

At an event in October 2026 I heard misconceptions that are now common among developers who use open source every day without having grown up in its culture, and What open source is, and what it is not corrects six of them. Anybody can change it, so nobody controls it: no, a few maintainers decide what goes in, and the control is real; it is just visible. Open source means anybody contributes: apart from a small minority of popular projects, almost every project is run by a handful of people, and that is its normal shape. It is more secure, or less secure, than proprietary software: neither; the metric is whether somebody had the time, and was paid, to review it. It costs nothing: somebody is always paying, and in one 2024 survey around 60% of maintainers described themselves as unpaid hobbyists. Open weights are open source: no, weights are a compiled binary. And the sixth is the one I just confessed to.

Through all of it, one sentence from 2018 is the one I would keep. When I see an application given away for free, as in zero cost rather than as in freedom, "I always think 'What is their agenda?'" Open source is a way of thinking about code in which there is no lock-in on the user by the creator.

The fork next door

Here is why that matters to a company, and to anyone deciding where to work. In a healthy startup everything is open source, so the technology is not the moat; customers stay for the service, the quality and the trust. Now ask what happens when one corner goes wrong. In a closed company, whoever holds control decides. In an open source company every corner has an exit, and the exit takes the technology with it. If the founders stop executing, investors can fund a team to run the same code next door. If a new owner changes direction or the licence, the founders can leave with the code, as long as no broad non-compete stops them. If anyone lets the customers down, the customers can move, as long as their data moves with them. The fork next door lays the triangle out.

Forks are rare and serious: in a study of 220 significant forks, only 7 merged back, and the famous ones that took hold all followed a breakdown, a new owner, a governance fight, a licence change. The reverse case is just as instructive. MariaDB was created in 2009 to protect MySQL from its new owners, and MySQL kept shipping, so the fork stayed a hedge. When I counted the commits to MySQL's main repository they had fallen from about 8,900 in 2019 to about 2,800 in 2025, which is the moment the argument predicts the fork should start to grow. That is a prediction, and it can be checked. So the real defence is not a licence or a patent. It is execution, and in the agent era, speed. The moat is a rate, not a wall: a competitor who forks today gets your position, not your velocity.

The security argument has moved too. Eric Raymond's line in The Cathedral and the Bazaar was "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow", and the honest answer was always that the eyeballs were not there: open source had the code in view and no budget for the slow reviews that find the hard bugs. Models can now run deep security reviews, over and over, on every change, at a cost that used to buy one consultant for a day. The eyeballs get cheap tells one project's year through its maintainer's own posts. curl's bug bounty had found 87 confirmed vulnerabilities over its life, and by 2025 "Not even one in twenty was real", so it ended. Then careful tools landed real bugs, "somewhere between two and three hundred bugfixes". Then a frontier scan found one vulnerability, "yes, as in singular one", of low severity. That is a project getting harder to break, wave by wave. The thing open source lacked was paid attention, and that is what just became abundant. It is why I think open source is about to become the most secure software we have.

Why it is easy to throw your own technology away

There is a quieter benefit, and it is the one I feel most in my own work. When everything I build is open source, I do not have a moat I have to keep using. There is no part of the stack I built that I need to protect from being replaced, and I am usually the first to try to replace it when something better appears. I wrote a library called Memory-FS; if the browser and a vault do its job for an application, the application stops using it. The habit is to ask of every component whether it is still earning its place, and open source removes the one bad reason to say yes: that I built it. Building blocks leans on that habit throughout.

The 2018 chapter made the personal version of the same point. Release your code and your future self can still reach it; move companies and you can still use what you built.

Your first pull request

Now the part for the person looking for a job. In 2018 I wrote that a great way to get a job in a company is to contribute to an open source project hosted by that company, and the OWASP chapter put it more plainly: most projects, even the big ones, are maintained by a couple of people, and "ALL projects are desperate for contributors and help!" The chapter on getting the job said "Act like you are part of the company", and Act as the job you want adds the 2026 version: if you are looking for a job, you have time, so do the work in public, where it can be linked, so that the people who want to help you can forward it.

Here is what a good first contribution looks like now. On 16 October 2026, late in the evening, Mondweep Chakravorty opened a pull request against an agent framework with 9,625 commits on its main branch. It added one page, 83 lines of documentation, describing his adapter: a behaviour policy compiled into the framework's own rules, gating memory checkpoints into an encrypted vault. The maintainer merged it the next afternoon. Our first PR in Ruflo puts the pieces side by side, and the lesson is in why it got in so quickly. No check ran and no formal review was recorded; what got it in was the page being specific about what it claims. It pinned the exact commits it depends on. It listed its evidence and its gaps in the same breath: "Semantic quality is NOT validated." It said what it was not: a documentation-only change. A page that does not oversell can be merged in under a day.

That is the bar, and it has risen since 2018. The small fixes a newcomer used to send can now be done by anyone with a model, and should be. curl's contribution rules now say that if you asked an AI tool to find problems, you must reveal that in your report. So a first pull request today carries its provenance: what you did, what you checked, what you did not. The 2018 outline GitHub Flow was five bullets I never wrote up, "Learn how to do Forks, Commits and Pull Requests" among them; the mechanics take an afternoon with an agent beside you, and Your first workspace covers them.

I should say where this came from, because ideas have a lineage. My own shift into an open source mindset happened, as the 2018 chapter records, "20 years ago after I read" The Cathedral and the Bazaar, and the lesson I quoted then still stands: "Release early. Release often. And listen to your customers." Linus Torvalds built two of the things all of this stands on, Linux and Git, and Git is the shape of an argument I keep making: a tool that decentralises trust changes how people work together. OWASP is where I learnt that knowledge compounds fastest in the open; the London chapter started over beers in a pub, and some of my best friends in the industry came from it. The 2018 chapter on Creative Commons quoted Tim O'Reilly, "For a typical author, obscurity is a far greater threat than piracy".

For Dinis your own first pull request to someone else's project, and what it led to; the 2018 chapter says the usual outcome is nothing, and a reader will want the counter-example from your life.

The excuse I hear from careful graduates is the one I heard in 2018: the code is not good enough yet. Put the licence on it, send the small, honest pull request, and let the people who want to help you find something they can link.
