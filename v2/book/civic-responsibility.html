Civic responsibility

The 2018 book had an outline called Civic Responsibility that I never wrote up. Its notes were six lines. As a developer you have a major civic responsibility. Code is law. You are one of the most important pieces of the puzzle. You can choose where you work and whether your code has a positive or negative impact in the world, and if you cannot choose that at the moment, make sure you are actively looking to change your job. Give the risks you create to the business owners, and make them accountable for the decisions they are making.

I am writing that chapter now, with my own country, because that is where I have made this argument for sixteen years, in public, with slides, and the record of what came true and what did not is a better teacher than a list of principles.

Lisbon, 2010 and 2016

On 17 December 2010 I gave the keynote at OWASP's IBWAS conference in Lisbon: 168 slides, five proposed focuses and twelve proposed actions for government and industry. Allow ethical hacking, publish security reviews as score cards, introduce liability for software, and "'Proper' SSL support for 50% of websites", among others. It ended by arguing that Portugal, which I described as depressed and with "nothing to lose", could become the Switzerland of hacking. What I asked for "doesn't cost any money"; "It only takes courage, focus and commitment".

Sixteen years later, more of it came true than I expected, though mostly from Brussels rather than Lisbon. From 9 December 2026 the EU's Product Liability Directive brings software under strict, no-fault liability; my slide asked only for government and listed companies, and the law went further. Europe has its own vulnerability database, run by ENISA since May 2025, and since 11 September 2026 the Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers to report actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours. HTTPS is the norm, far past my 50 percent.

What I got wrong was the mechanism. I thought fear, a big enough disaster, would move governments quickly. What moved them was a decade of slow, unglamorous regulation: liability and reporting duties, not a single catastrophe. And the part about Portugal itself, an open, ethical security industry as a way out of a depressed economy, is still mostly to do.

On 11 November 2016 I gave the keynote at BSidesLisbon, Hacking Portugal, 103 slides, and three weeks later published it as a book on Leanpub, 75 pages, whose page still says 95 percent complete. Its first lines are the premise of this chapter: "If the word 'hack' means to solve problems, then 'Hacking Portugal' means to find innovative ways to solve existing problems." The ideas were blunt. Make the Portuguese network hostile to insecure code. Replace the old compulsory military service with a Portuguese Hacking Service for 15 to 21 year olds. A hackathon league as famous as football, with the kids, the clubs and the pride. OpenSource.pt: all code paid for by government released under an open-source licence. A Ministry of Code.

Ten years on, the regulation arrived from Europe, close enough to the Clear Software Act on slide 83. The Hacking Service, the league and the Ministry of Code have not happened, as far as I know. The best news is in the spirit of OpenSource.pt: in 2026 Portugal released Amália, a national language model for European Portuguese, as open source, built by a consortium of universities with 5.5 million euros of EU recovery money, published with its dataset and code.

And the biggest change is one I did not see in 2016. Then, learning to hack meant learning to code first. Now a sixteen-year-old can explore a system by talking to it, which Explorer coding, and the handover called explorer coding. A Hacking Service in 2026 would teach young people to direct agents against their own country's systems, under a mandate that says what they may and may not touch. The talk's central claim, that a small country can choose to lead in code, is more possible than it was, because everyone who can brief an agent can now build.

Judo for small countries

The conversation about a national AI strategy keeps circling the biggest model, the most GPUs, the largest data centre. On a Wardley map those sit at the far right, in commodity, with a lead held by companies that have invested amounts a small country's whole technology budget would not match. It is the wrong end of the map for anyone without the capital.

Look at the other end instead. Put a person at the top: a developer in Cluj, a designer in Porto, a product team in Tallinn, selling a service to a buyer abroad. What stops them is not talent, energy or price. It is forming a company, hiring across a border, being paid across Europe, working in a language the buyer reads, a contract, a tax form valid on both sides. None of that is the service. All of it is plumbing, and in most of Europe each piece is custom built by the person who needs it, which means built badly, late, and at a cost that falls on the wrong side. In the last Eurostat figures with a breakdown, for 2021, fewer than one in ten firms in the single market sells across its own borders online. Commerce does not happen, most of the time, because of attrition.

We understand that a government builds roads, because they enable everybody. The shop that opens on the new road did not pay for the road; it paid taxes. Yet we ask every person who wants to sell a service out of a small country to build their own road first. In the age of agents, that plumbing is infrastructure in the road sense, and a state that builds it enables all of its people at once. Estonia did it: a person anywhere can become an e-resident and form an Estonian company online, the programme's dashboard lists more than 142,000 e-residents and more than 43,000 companies, and underneath sits X-Road, open source under the MIT licence, so another country can take the road rather than draw it again.

The plumbing has to be open source and run on open schemas, because that is what turns infrastructure into sovereignty. A road with a toll gate owned by one company is lock-in with tarmac. What the state pays for should belong to the commons, not so everyone changes the production system, but so everyone can see it, run it and leave with their data.

Then put the money where the market is still at genesis. Most work does not need a frontier model and should not use one. That means small models, and many of them: not one Portuguese but the Portuguese of Portugal and of Brazil, of Angola, of Cape Verde, of Mozambique, each with its culture in the training data and not just its spelling. A public model is a public road. And treat constraints as the input, not the excuse: DeepSeek's own report says its V3 model was trained in 2.788 million GPU hours on export-restricted chips whose interconnect was deliberately throttled, with custom code written around the constraint.

Sovereignty is the ability to move

I am learning to play padel, and the striking thing about a good player is how much time they have. They read the ball, they know where it will be after it comes off the glass, and they are already there. If I move after it has bounced off the back wall, there is no hope. The ball today is the frontier model and the data centre; where the ball is going is the ability to move: workloads, data and the actions agents take, from one provider to another, when you decide and not on the day a supplier decides for you.

That is my definition. Technology sovereignty is the ability to move, almost liquidity. The honest test of most organisations is that they cannot do it, not between clouds and often not even within one, because the infrastructure was hard-coded, created by clicks, and never deployed in a second place. That is an engineering gap, not a policy gap.

The test is a Wardley map with one question. Put the customer at the top, the service, the application, the data, and under them the products and commodities you buy. For each dot you do not control: take it away, what happens? Follow the edges up. If the service stops, you have found the problem, and it belongs on the risk register with a name next to it, all the way to the board. That is what the 2018 outline meant by giving the risks you create to the business owners. Draw the dependency, show what happens when it goes, and make the person who owns the decision own it.

Most sovereignty efforts never start because they set the bar at an alternative as good as today. Nobody gets from zero to a hundred. The bar should be: it works, for the minimum viable company, the priority customers and the services that must never stop, slower and dearer if need be. Run it in QA, all the time, on twins of the dependencies you do not control, so that every small problem is found on a Tuesday rather than during an outage. Ask whether it can move before asking whether it is cheap.

And it has to be open source, because even a European supplier is one acquisition away from not being one, in either direction. Control that survives a change of owner is the only control that counts.

What this asks of you

Code is law, in the plain sense that what you build decides what people can and cannot do, and in 2026 what your agents can and cannot do. You can choose where you work and whether your code has a positive or negative impact, and if you cannot choose that today, be actively looking. Give the risks you create to the people who own the decisions, drawn on a map they can read.

If you are in a small country, you are not behind. You are at the end of the map where the market still exists. The move is judo: use the weight of the commodity models, clouds and rails rather than trying to match it, build the plumbing once and in the open, buy the alternative before the day you need it, and regulate what settled rather than what is still moving.

For Dinis the register notes that a Portuguese edition, or both languages, is your open question, and this chapter is the one it bears on most. Which is it, and does the Hacking Portugal material stay here or move to the new edition of that book?

In 2010 I said what I asked for would cost no money, only courage, focus and commitment. In 2016 I said to hack means to solve problems. Both still hold. What has changed is who can do the hacking: in 2016 the people who had learned to code, now anyone who can brief an agent, which makes the civic responsibility wider, not narrower. You are one of the most important pieces of the puzzle. Act like it.
