In 2018 I wrote a short chapter called *Be a founder*. It said that the single thing you personally control when you go to work is your attitude to it, and it asked you to imagine you were employee number four and cared deeply about the company. Then it asked five questions. If I were a founder of the company, department or section I work in now, with the responsibilities I have at the moment: how would I behave every day? What needs to be done now that will make a big difference? What can I do that will help? What would I do differently? What values and principles would I fight for? And then the question under all of them: what is stopping you from doing just that? Who is telling you not to? "At the moment it is just you!"

I still mean every word of that. The more you care and the more you behave like a founder, the more value you add and the more valuable you become. But in 2018 I meant it as a way of behaving inside somebody else's company. In 2026 I also mean it literally, because the thing that stopped most people from founding anything has just become cheap.

## The playbook that worked

Look at the technology companies that did well in the last ten or twenty years, and at the many more that were funded or bought, and a pattern repeats. Someone very clever and very ambitious finds a gap in a market. Often they experienced it personally; often they did the famous hundred calls with stakeholders and asked what hurts, which already tells you something, because they were people who could get those calls. Then they raised money on belief, hired the people they did not have, including the subject-matter experts, and built a company on top.

I have watched this many times in cybersecurity, and it worked. The startups displaced incumbents that had a much better starting position: the customers, the domain knowledge, sometimes the money. What the incumbents lacked was the structure, the workflows and, to be fair, the technology culture. The talent was often already inside them. Adrian Cockcroft, then at Netflix, tells of a Fortune 100 CTO who complained that Netflix had a superstar development team and his company did not. His answer: "Netflix hired them from you, and got out of their way."

This is not a criticism. Those companies changed industries, and sometimes new blood is the only way. But the playbook has a second act. The disruptors became the incumbents, and much of what they built is now the software everyone complains about. Workers in three Fortune 500 companies were measured toggling between applications roughly 1,200 times a day. Security teams juggle an average of 83 tools from 29 vendors. Many of those tools were the disruptors once.

## The experts were always the better bet

The myth of the young outsider was never the whole story. The largest study I know, of 2.7 million US founders, found that the mean age at founding of the fastest-growing one-in-a-thousand new ventures is 45, and that prior experience in the specific industry predicts much greater rates of success: founders with three or more years in the same industry reached top-tier growth at twice the rate of those with none.

What held the experts back was not knowledge. It was access: to capital, to engineers, to the long, expensive build before anything could be shown. That is exactly the part that got cheap. With generative AI, and agents in particular, much of the execution that used to need a funded team can be done by one person who knows exactly what to build. The data is moving the same way: the share of new US startups with a solo founder rose from 23.7 percent in 2019 to 36.3 percent in the first half of 2025, and the top hundred AI companies on Stripe reached a million dollars of annualised revenue in a median of 11.5 months. Those are the fastest, not the average, and some such claims are a promoter's own. But the direction is clear. The millions that used to be the price of starting are no longer the price of starting.

Three examples from the newsroom article *The expert founders*, and I should say that two are people I help and the third is me. I come from application security and from working as a CISO; sgit.ai and RiskMandate are my security knowledge turned into a company, where the agents write most of the code and the briefs, the buyers and the risk model are mine. A consultant knee surgeon in London is building a platform for knee health; he knew knees, and the patients and clinicians who need the product were his long before there was one. A transcriber is building legal transcription software, "Built by a Transcriber, for Transcription Professionals". None of them is a hundred-call outsider. Each comes from the field, has the contacts in it, and is focused on one thing. That focus used to be a weakness in front of an investor who wanted a huge market. It is now the advantage, because the build is no longer what stops them.

If you have just finished a degree, you may read this and think it is about other people, older people with a field. Read it the other way round. The expert can build first now; the question is what you are the expert in, or will be. Three years in one industry doubles the odds. You have time to get them.

And it favours where you are. It is often said that American investors back the vision and European investors want to see it working. There is myth on both sides, but the data has a version of it: European companies raise at higher revenue, at lower valuations, with smaller targets. I think that scepticism is exactly what this generation needs, because money too early is one of the most reliable ways to kill a startup. A study of about 3,200 high-growth internet startups found that 74 percent failed through premature scaling. An expert founder who can build the first version with agents, show it working, and then raise for scale rather than for discovery is exactly the founder sceptical money is good for. The gap is real at the other end, and I will not hide it: the Draghi report puts the EU's share of global venture funds raised at 5 percent against 52 percent in the US, and close to 30 percent of the unicorns founded in Europe between 2008 and 2021 moved their headquarters abroad. Europe is weak at scaling. My argument is that the first part, the part that now matters most, is where it is strong.

## Do they miss it?

Most advice about starting a company is about raising money. This is not that. It is the operating model I use, and it has three pillars, in an order that matters.

Be profitable. Investors should be talking to you, not the other way round. Open source everything.

The first pillar starts with the word ship, and the word has been worn smooth, so be careful with it. Shipping does not mean a proof of concept, a demo, or a video of a demo. It means a thing somebody else can go and use, on their own, and get value from. It can be held together with tape. Nobody using it cares how it was built, and neither should you, yet. *Shipped means the user has it* said the same thing from the builder's side.

Then hand it to people for free, for a short window. The feedback starts the moment somebody who is not you has the thing. And then comes the part almost nobody does deliberately: take it away. The trial expired. It is not available right now. Then ask the only question that matters. Do they miss it?

If they shrug and go back to their day, you do not have a product yet. You have something people will accept when it is free, which is a much weaker signal than it feels like from the inside. If they come back and ask for it, you have something, and that is the moment the whole model turns on.

Now two questions, and people routinely answer only the first. Is the price one they are happy to pay? And is that price profitable for you? Revenue comes first; if you cannot get revenue at all, that is the problem to solve, and it is not a marketing problem. One trap here: do not reach for a subscription by default. Almost every time any of us buys something, we are buying future value, and the research says we are bad at it. Members of three gyms on a flat monthly fee of over 70 dollars attended on average 4.3 times a month, paying more than 17 dollars a visit when a ten-visit pass would have cost them 10. Charging rent for something people are not using is a worse business than being paid when you deliver. The newsroom runs the opposite experiment: every reader starts with five pounds of credit, an article read to the end uses ten pence, the balance can go below zero and stay there, and nothing nags. By the time you are asked to pay, the value is in front of you. It only works where the cost of serving is close to nothing, which is true of a static site and of a lot of software; a business with a real cost per unit cannot let balances run negative for ever. The results are not in; the review is in December 2026.

The second pillar follows from the first. The worst possible time to raise money is before you are profitable, before you have the product, before you understand the fit, which is of course exactly when most companies raise. You are bargaining from weakness and both sides know it. The worst terms are not about money; they are about control, handed to people whose job is to make money and whose focus is now structurally misaligned with yours. The analogy I keep coming back to is music. Do not get signed before you have the album. Write the songs, record them in a bedroom studio, play locally, get people buying. Then take the deal, as somebody who already has an audience and does not strictly need one.

The third pillar surprises people, because it sounds like giving away the asset. Open source everything. The technology is not the moat, and believing it is will shape every decision badly; treating it as open forces you to find what is actually defensible, usually the data, the relationships, the distribution or the speed at which you ship. Code written to be read by strangers has cleaner boundaries. A team that has never deleted a component will defend one long after it should have gone. The customer can read the thing, so nobody has to trust a promise about what happens if you disappear. And it is a better exit for you: build proprietary and you accumulate a pile of technology you cannot take with you; build in the open and the work is still yours when you move on. You leave with your tools. *Open source* has the longer argument.

## There is a company in this

I want to end with a company, said plainly, so that this chapter is not only a way of thinking. It sits with the other business plans published for founders on sgit.ai, and I would be glad to see it built with us or without us.

Source code is a fractal semantic graph: layers within layers, each a graph with its own vocabulary, from what the user is trying to do, through the features, the components, the classes, the methods and the calls between them, down to the syntax tree. A code review today reads a text diff, which is at one altitude, the lines, and it is the wrong altitude for almost every question a reviewer has. Does this change what a user sees? Is this the fix it claims to be, or a refactor? What else can it reach? Did the new test assert the thing that was broken? None of those is a question about lines. If every layer is kept as files, every layer can be diffed: a refactor should move the bottom layers and leave the stories still; a bug fix should be visible at the top as a story that holds again; the blast radius of a change is the climb from the methods it touched to the stories that can reach them. The worked example is published as a vault: the sgit command-line tool, 377 classes and 1,111 methods, read as layered graphs with nothing run, including one real commit read upwards from the seven methods it changed to the nine commands and six user stories it can reach.

What the company sells is a code review that reads every layer. Attach it to a repository and it derives the graph once, keeps it current per commit, and on every change shows the reviewer the diff at each altitude, the fix-or-refactor shape, and the climb to the commands, interfaces and stories the change can reach. It keeps the graph as files in the customer's own repository or vault, so the customer owns the record. It runs the customer's own rules as queries, and it gets better on a codebase the longer it runs there, because every correction is a commit.

Who buys it is anyone generating code faster than they can review it, which this year is everyone, and in particular the teams whose review is a compliance requirement rather than a courtesy: regulated software, payments, health, anything with a threat model drawn once a year and never connected to the code. For those teams the threat model and the component layer are the same graph, and the sale is that they finally agree with each other.

What is defensible is the record and the loop, not the model. Models will be commodities; the graph of a particular codebase, corrected over a year by the people who know it, is not. The method is written down, the scripts are Apache-2.0, and a founder does not need my permission. They need a second language and a first customer.

If you are the person with the degree and the first language, that last sentence is written for you. That is a project a competent person could start on Monday. In 2018 I asked what was stopping you, and answered that it was just you. The answer has not changed. What has changed is how little else is in the way.
