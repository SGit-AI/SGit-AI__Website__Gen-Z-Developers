One morning in October 2026 I asked the agent that runs my newsroom to read the day's articles and tell me what it could build next. It came back with a long list, all of it possible: an inbox mode, reading modes, marks, automating the wire's daily run, more beats, scheduling, analytics. Every item was a reasonable thing to do, and a team of agents could have started on all of them by lunchtime. That is the problem this chapter is about. When the agents can go in every direction, where do you focus?

Direction is the scarce thing. Agents can build in any direction; what they cannot generate is the sense of what to build next and when to stop. Providing that is the job, and if you are hiring, it is the thing to hire for. Building stopped being the bottleneck the day anyone could brief an agent. Choosing is what is left.

## Where the next step moves the needle most

My rule has a name I gave it years ago, when I was a CISO, and the newsroom has an article under that name. At any moment there are many things that could be done. Most are blocked on something: a decision, a dependency, a person. Some are not blocked but would not change much. A few are not blocked and would change a lot, for the people who use the thing and for everything built after. Work on those, and only those, until they stop being that. Not where the biggest problem is; where the next step moves the needle most.

It is a rule about where, not what, and it does not require knowing how the whole problem will be solved. I often do not. By the time I get there I will have better tooling, a better understanding and a better view, because every step produces those three things. So you start somewhere, and the somewhere can be slightly wrong, because the first step's job is not to be right. It is to produce the second step's information.

Steve Jobs said the same thing about a life, at Stanford in 2005: "you can't connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backwards." The newsroom gave me a three-day example. A reading meter led to paying by how far you read, which made personas worth keeping, which made sharing worth encrypting, which needed a vault per reader, which needed a passkey. The passkey design had a door between the key and the master; a review took it out as a layer too many; a lab found after midnight that a phone over QR returned different bytes, and the door went back, as a door per path. Two reversals in twelve hours, both right, both cheap, because the design had been shipped as something you could press rather than argued as a document. Backwards, the sequence looks inevitable. Forwards, at each step there were five choices.

The same rule explains why the newsroom solved a problem my company could not. Where to keep a user's master vault key had resisted six months of attempts, each of which failed my simplicity test. The newsroom solved it in two days, because it had readers on every device I own and a workflow that was failing me in a way I could feel. When there is not enough momentum to need a solution, my instinct is not to build one. "Focus on one thing only" is good advice that assumes progress runs in a straight line. It rarely does.

The 2018 book had a draft on deadlines that called many of them "an arbitrary point in the future when somebody would like to see something done", and a draft called *Start with why*, whose point was not to challenge everything but to understand why we are doing something. The second matters more now: the better I explain the why in a brief, the better the result.

## Knowing when to stop

The hardest call in most work is not what to do next but when to stop, and this is the part agents lack most. Their range is the feature. They can go in any direction, which is close to what we call creativity in a person. But an entity that can go anywhere has no natural reason to stop anywhere. You ask it to fix a small thing and it fixes a bigger one, because it can. It sees twenty things wrong and has a go at all of them, without the perspective to know that nineteen do not matter, or matter later, or are someone else's.

People have the same problem, with less range. When security stopped being whack-a-mole, the best teams treated it as a class of bug and ran out of real work, which was great. Then some kept going. I was once called into a meeting where security champions and developers were debating whether GUIDs were random enough: a movie plot, in Bruce Schneier's phrase for the dramatic threats we imagine instead of the ordinary ones that happen. I sat in other meetings where development teams hit every KPI and the business did not move. What I found myself doing in those rooms was giving people perspective: yes, that is technically possible, and it does not matter here, because of who our attackers are, what else is in place and what we are protecting.

That perspective is much of what seniority is: knowing who the attackers are, which phase the business is in, where the bottleneck is, and what good enough looks like. The 2018 chapter on the brain asked "Who is actually making the decisions in your head?", and described a slow kind of thinking and a fast kind that knows without articulating. Seniority is the fast kind, backed by a bigger picture. An agent has the speed and not the picture, so the picture has to be designed in: a brief that explains the thinking and not only the task; a mandate that only reaches what the job needs; memory that holds the strategy and the threats; a stop named before the work begins; one kind of work per step; and shipping often enough that users tell you whether it mattered. None of these is a rule that says do less. Each puts the bigger picture where the decision is made.

The junior version is this. Name the stop before you start: a time, a budget, or a result that counts as good enough. Write down which phase you are in, exploring, settling or planning, because the right amount of polish follows from it. And give yourself explicit permission to leave things undone, and write down what you left and why.

## Where a person belongs

The 2018 book had an outline on one-way and two-way doors, with a story I attributed to someone at Google: "My metric for approve or not a project is based on how easy it is to roll back the changes (and its impact of real customers)". I still cannot say who said it. Jeff Bezos wrote the idea down in his 2015 letter to shareholders: some decisions are "consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible", to be made slowly; most are "changeable, reversible", and the mistake large organisations make is to use the heavy process for both.

> **For Dinis:** the Google executive's test appears in the 2018 outline and again in *How cheap is it to undo*, both times from memory; if the source can be found the book should name it, and if not, say so once.

Now apply the test to every output an agent produces. Not "do I trust the agent", which has no answer, but: if this is wrong, how cheap is it to undo, and what does it break before I do? On 13 October 2026 my newsroom shipped fourteen releases, every commit made by an agent, with nobody approving each one before it went live. The same day the twelve agents that run my correspondence drafted every email and sent none. That is not a contradiction. An article is a two-way door: a correction is a new version, and the next reader gets the fixed page. An email in my name is a one-way door.

Someone shaping a roundtable in London asked me whether the human in the loop is a safety net or a bottleneck. A person checking every output is both, and mostly neither. It is a bottleneck because it does not scale, and a weak safety net because people approve almost everything they are shown: Anthropic measured that its coding agent's users approve 93% of permission prompts. The question is not whether there is a human in the loop but where. Put the person where an output cannot be undone and the blast radius is large: sending in my name, typing a secret, spending money, deleting what has no backup. Everywhere else, use controls that do not get tired: a validator, a second reader the agent cannot skip, a policy row enforced outside the model, and a record read afterwards.

Blast radius means what it would cost the business if a row of the agent's reach were used in full, today. It is defined over the reach, not the mandate, because whoever takes an agent over, through a poisoned document or an injected instruction, inherits its reach and ignores its mandate. It has a second axis the industry usually leaves out: whether there is a way back. And it changes while the action does not. Picture an agent whose cleanup step drops a staging table. The first time the table is nearly empty. The second time it holds a week of test data. The third time it holds six weeks of real records, and it is the incident. Same row, three times. If someone had been reading the footprint, what the agent actually did, the review could say: this is not the first time. The 2018 draft on chaos engineering said security had been doing this for ages, and that the first step was to understand and visualise what is going on. It still is.

When the person does review, the review should be worth more than a yes. I once received a permission prompt asking me to add label 756-459-3214. Which message, which label, why? There was no way for that to be a good decision or a bad one; it was a click. A decider with no options, no context, no time and no authority is not exercising agency; they are holding liability for a decision the system made, and the accountability belongs to whoever designed the decision point. Above that line, a review becomes a quality step: each draft is a chance to fix whatever produced it, the record, the brief, the rule, rather than the draft itself. And the record moves the line. Trust in my email agents is a record of drafts I did not have to change. When that record is long enough for one kind of message, that kind is a candidate to leave my hands, by a decision written into the policy, with an owner and a date.

## Agents decide, people answer

An early user of my agentic email workflow wrote back with the right question. If the agents only gather and draft, and I review and send, does presenting them as a team imply an agency they do not have? Would anyone ever need to talk to the agents around me?

Two things are true at once. These are not sentient beings; they are mirrors that can simulate any emotion, which is why we anthropomorphise them, as people did with Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA in 1966, when his own secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it in private. And since models started running in loops and calling tools, they make real decisions: every tool call is a choice. What does not travel with the decision is responsibility. It stays with whoever granted the reach and set the mandate. The moment of authorisation is the grant. Nobody legitimate needs to go around me; the only party who would try is an attacker with an injected instruction, which is why the agents never send.

The best analogy is an employee with a job description: some agency, inside a role. An Agent Behaviour Policy is that job description, published, and the rows that matter most are the prohibitions: do not send an email directly; do not edit files another agent owns; do not create or change a scheduled task; do not attack other websites, even though you can; speak on your own behalf, never on mine. Most of those are still hope, a rule the model is asked to keep. But a prohibition that is written down and then broken is a different thing in a dispute from no prohibition at all. Ralf Thomas asked on LinkedIn: "If an employee wrecks a client's systems, the company pays. Why should AI Agents be any different?" My hypothesis is that towards the world the deployer still answers, and between deployer and vendor the written policy is what turns "you allowed it" into "your product ignored what it was told".

The 2018 book had a draft on civic responsibility that said "code is law", and that developers should give the risks they create to business owners and make them accountable for the decisions they are making. The behaviour policy is that sentence, written down for an agent. Fluent writing is being devalued, as everything that becomes a commodity is. What remains valuable is judgement, and someone who owns it: who decided this is true, who decided to send it, who will answer for it. That is the person who gets paid, and the person I would hire.
