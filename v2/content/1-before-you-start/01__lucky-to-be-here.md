I discover things I can do. That is the sentence I keep coming back to when someone asks what it is like to work the way I work now. I discover workflows. I follow an idea, and I can execute it, often the same afternoon. The feeling is not relief, and it is not fear. It is closer to wonder, and I want it to be the first thing in this book, because it is the first thing I would want you to feel.

I heard a woman on a podcast describe the same thing: the wonder that happens with generative AI, the feeling of discovering what you can do. I recognised it at once. I cannot now say who she was, so I will not put a name to it.

## How the first edition started

The 2018 book did not start with wonder. It started with a slide I had shown to a room of young people in 2017, and a question the book repeats on its first page: "Here is the slide that started it all in 2017, how many logos do you recognize?" Very few. My point was that each of those logos was a change in how technology worked, and nobody had told them the story behind it.

I still think the history matters. But the book that followed went too technical, too deep, too soon. Parts 2 and 3 were nineteen chapters of technologies to know. The argument was at the two ends, and the middle was a list.

What I had actually wanted is in that book's introduction, under the heading "Overwhelming curiosity": moments when you "follow link after link about a particular topic" and think it is awesome. The 2018 book called that place the zone: "If you are lucky enough to find yourself in this place, congratulations! You just found 'the zone'." The second edition starts there. Not with what you should know, but with what you can now do, and the wonder of discovering it.

In 2018 I also asked, of those logos, "Why is the **why** important?", and I had an outline for a chapter called *Start with why*, after Simon Sinek's golden circle: why before how before what. The outline was never written. So let me start with the why of this book, in one sentence.

## You are lucky to be here

Whoever is reading this in 2026 or 2027 is ridiculously privileged to be living at this moment in time, in the middle of a huge transition in society, while the pieces are still landing.

I know that is not what you hear. What you hear is that AI took the jobs before you got there, and that the people who moved into AI two years ago are already tired of it. I tried to read one of those essays this month, in Business Insider, under the headline "I thought working in AI would put me ahead of the curve. Two years in, I've never felt more behind and tired of the topic." I got three paragraphs before a subscription box covered the page, so I cannot tell you what it argued. In Europe in particular, I think young people are being fed a lot of fear about AI, and are not embracing it the way they should. That is my reading, not a measurement, and this edition still owes you a proper answer to it.

I am not going to tell you that none of the fear is right. Projects will be cancelled. Money will be lost. People who promised too much will be found out. Every transition has had all of that. But the fear is what you hear; it is not where this book starts, and I do not think it is where you should start either.

So here is my position, and it is a position, not a forecast: ignore the doom, because nobody knows what is going to happen. Every time we had one of these technological transformations, people said the same things, and they always said this time is going to be different. And each time the paths evolved: industries changed completely, jobs disappeared and appeared, and the people who learned the new tools early did well out of it.

## The transformations I have lived through

I have seen several of these in my own lifetime, each one a revolution that changed whole industries. The dates are the commonly recorded ones.

- The home computer, my ZX Spectrum, in the early 1980s. A computer became something you could own, open and program.
- The BBS, in the 1980s and early 1990s. Text messages and chat between strangers, over a phone line.
- The internet, in the 1990s. Everything published, linked and searchable.
- The mobile phone, in the late 1990s, and the smartphone from 2007. The network in your pocket.
- The cloud, from the mid 2000s. Servers you rent by the hour instead of buy.
- The apps, from 2008, with the app stores. Software delivered to everyone, instantly.
- Generative AI, from November 2022. Software you can talk to, and that can build software.

The 2018 book had an outline for the second of those, *BBS and modems*, with a story I still have to tell properly: blowing up my home's telephone system trying to connect to one. I had to disconnect the phone line by hand and remake the plug to get the modem on it.

The only difference this time, I sometimes joke, is that this one is televised. It is far more visible, because of the money involved, because of the players, and because of how much of the stack it touches at once. In the past these changes took longer to land. This one will also take longer than the headlines say; it just feels as if it is happening all at once.

I lived through the dot-com crash, and it was the same pattern: enormous hype, then a crash. The Nasdaq peaked in March 2000 and had lost roughly four fifths of its value by October 2002, and the internet still changed everything. We might have a crash or a correction this time too; nobody can say when, or whether. There is a circle of money flying around, and the numbers are bigger than they have ever been. None of that makes the transformation less real. A crash would change who makes money; it would not undo what people can now do.

My own last two years are the counter-story to the burnout essay. In *Two years in, the opposite* I put it this way: "I have been doing AI for the last two years and a bit, and I have never felt more productive." I have never felt happier at work, or more empowered. It is not easy. I am doing a startup, things fail every other week, and you could tell the same two years as a miserable story with a failure a week. I do not see it like that. There is an anti-AI mood at the moment, and stories of burnout feed it; stories like mine should be told too, and this book is where I tell mine. It is one person's account, not a study.

A couple of years ago I remember wondering whether we had missed the boat. The cloud was stabilising, things were getting more boring, and in my own world, cybersecurity, it felt as if all the big things had been done. Now the pieces of the puzzle are landing again. This is the biggest opportunity of your career: the moment when you can still be a player, before things stabilise, as they always do, until the next big thing. In 2018 I wrote that the next revolution in technology would decide "whether you are going to be a player or a pawn", and I pointed readers at learning how to program machine learning. I was right about the revolution and wrong about the skill: the bigger shift turned out to be learning how to use it, and how to direct agents.

## Technology that finally works for you

For a long time technology has not worked for you. Your generation in particular was handed a technology stack designed to hook you, and to make you a passive user. There was some creating, but mostly you were a consumer, living in a world of advertising, attention and dopamine. I believe that will one day be treated the way tobacco is today; that is my belief, and the evidence is still being gathered. The 2018 book had a draft chapter on dopamine that already called phones' attention-grabbing activities "the new smoking".

My generation was privileged in a different way: we could build things. A whole generation grew up programming, building computers, mixing parts. A computer was not something that just arrived; you could take it apart. You were handed technology to use, not technology to build. Now you can build again.

Here is what that looks like in practice. For twenty years the instinct in software was that a custom interface is expensive, so it is reserved for the product and everyone else gets the generic one: the inbox, the ticket, the form. In *Custom UIs are not the exception* I argued that this is now backwards: "a custom interface per message, per thread, per topic, per question is not an exception. It is how interfaces get made." When an interface costs an afternoon, you stop asking whether it is worth building and start asking which moment it is for. The tools can be shaped to you, instead of you being shaped to the tools.

The wonder underneath that has a name in my influences. Bret Victor's talk *Inventing on Principle*, from 2012, says that a creator needs to see what they are making, as they make it. Every step between you and the thing you are making, a compile, a deploy, a refresh, is a place where an idea can die. Generative AI removes a lot of those steps, and that is a big part of what I feel when I say I discover things I can do. The storyboards in this book are an example: they are redrawn the moment their data changes. The 2018 book had an outline for that talk too, with a note that is very much me: "print this and read it".

None of this is only for the creative ones. It is not that you have to be creative, and if you are not, you are left out. It is that you can now scale the things you are not good at. You can be helped. You can have technology work for you.

And it is why I am an optimist about where technology goes, rather than about any one product. In 2018 I recommended Kevin Kelly's *The Inevitable*, because of the way he maps what is likely to happen next: technology evolves with tendencies of its own. Which of his books I would hand you first is a question I have not settled. The frame of this book, which *Where do you land?* introduces, is built on that kind of seeing: things move in a direction, and the people who can see the direction can be early.

## How to read this book

Read it on paper, with a pen. I mean that literally. Books, and narratives, are still the best way we have to take in information, and your brain takes it in much better from a page you can mark. If you are reading this as a PDF, print it. Highlight, underline, cross things out, write in the margin, and if you want to tell me what you wrote, I would like to hear it. The 2018 book had a chapter called *Pen and Paper* that said "analogue techniques like the pen and paper are actually better technologies for creating and capturing ideas." I still think so, and I say so knowing that it is my experience rather than a finding; the research on reading on paper and on screens is still to be read before I say more.

Read it somewhere you can give it your undivided attention. That is a skill, and the best professionals in the world have it. The phone you were handed was designed to take it from you.

Then use the rest of the book the way *Where do you land?* describes: find your centre of gravity first, and read the chapters marked for it before the others. *Connect the dots backwards* is about how to learn, and it says something I want you to hold on to from this first page: your path will only make sense looking back, so you do not have to see it now. You have to start.

That, in the end, is why the title says developer. Craftspeople made their own tools. In the past they did it out of necessity; now you can do it again, because the tools can be made to fit you, and because, as *Everyone who can brief an agent is a developer* argues, the bar to making them has dropped to the floor. It is still your name on the work, and it is still your thinking. You think with the AI; you do not delegate your thinking to it.

You are lucky to be here. Not because it will be easy, but because the pieces are still landing, and you can still be the one who places some of them.
