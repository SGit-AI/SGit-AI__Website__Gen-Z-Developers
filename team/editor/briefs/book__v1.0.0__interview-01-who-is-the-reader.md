# Interview 01: who is the reader, and what does the book ask of them

*A brief for the editor, from the build brief Generation Z Developers, second edition (18 October 2026) and the article Generation Z Developers, again. Version interviewed: book v1.0.0. Method: ten hard questions for RiskMandate; one question at a time, every answer challenged, until the set is coherent. The transcript is kept verbatim in the vault's `interviews/01/` and copied here when signed off.*

## How to run it

- Ask one question. Wait for the answer, by voice memo or in the session. Challenge it with the challenge written below, or a better one the answer invites. Follow the answer where it goes. Only then the next question.
- Do not summarise during the interview. Summaries come after, in a separate file.
- Stop when the narrative is coherent, not when the questions run out. If it takes two hours, it takes two hours.

## The questions

1. **Who exactly is the reader you hand this to, and what do they do differently the week after reading it?**
   Why: the whole edition is for one kind of reader, and "Generation Z" is a name, not a description.
   Expected: someone coming out of university or looking for their first or next job; someone Dinis meets who should get into AI.
   Challenge: the article also says it is "the manual for getting a job" for anyone, and the brief adds people who do not yet work the way Dinis does. Those are three readers. Which one is the book written for, and what do the other two get?

2. **The 2018 cover says "question everything". What should a reader question about AI that the 2026 conversation does not?**
   Why: the seven lines on the cover stay; this one has to mean something specific now.
   Expected: the fear of AI young Europeans are fed; the idea that you need to build models.
   Challenge: "question everything" is also the licence to dismiss the book's own claims. What claim in this book should the reader question first?

3. **Where does a new developer start: as an explorer, a villager or a town planner? Can they choose?**
   Why: the frame of the edition is Wardley's; if it does not fit the reader's first week, it is decoration.
   Expected: start as an explorer, because that is what agents make cheap.
   Challenge: most first jobs are villager or town planner work. Is the advice to be an explorer, or to know which one you are being asked to be?

4. **You say young Europeans are fed fear about AI. Fear of what, from whom, and what is the honest answer to it?**
   Why: the article marks this as a reading, not a measured result; the book cannot say it without saying what it rests on.
   Expected: fear of losing jobs, from the press and from schools.
   Challenge: some of the fear is right (the first wave of hiring did go to the wrong people, some projects will be cancelled). Which fears does the book concede?

5. **Which 2018 chapter are you most proud of, and which would you cut?**
   Why: the reviewer's verdicts need the author's own first.
   Expected: proud of how to get a job; cut the technology lists.
   Challenge: the longest written chapter is Wallaby and NCrunch, filed under draft. Keep, cut or rewrite, and why?

6. **"Everyone is a developer" can sound like a slogan. What can't someone who only briefs agents do, that a 2018 developer could?**
   Why: the book's definition of developer widened; it has to say what it lost.
   Expected: they cannot read the code, debug it, or know when the agent is wrong.
   Challenge: then the book needs a chapter on exactly that. Which one is it, and how much code does the reader have to read?

7. **What is the first workflow the reader should run, end to end, on day one?**
   Why: hired for the workflow says pick one and do it end to end; the book must name it.
   Expected: a GitHub account, an agent, a site built from a voice memo (your own newsroom).
   Challenge: that takes an afternoon for Dinis. What does it take for the reader in question 1, and what do they have at the end that they can link?
