Generation X, 54. “Is this anything I have not read already, and is it actually a person's thinking or AI slop with a famous-ish name on it?”

Everybody in this study is invented. The readers, their names and every word attributed to them were written by a language model (Claude Sonnet, one agent per reader) reading the candidate's text. It is not reader research, and no sentence in it is evidence about a real person or a real market. What is evidence is the candidate's own text: every quote a reader cites is checked by the build, word for word, against the unit it cites, in the frozen file whose hash is recorded here.
Put it downCh. 41: How this book was made, and what you made

I came to hunt weak points and had what I needed: the chapters with the biggest claims are better sourced than I expected, but the book repeats itself, leans on the author's own products, and the final chapter is about the machine that made it.

Buy, at that pointno

There are ideas I had not read (ch 21, 25, 23) and it is honest, but the promised company chapters are missing, and I would be paying for repetition and product. Format: ebook. Would pay: nothing at £9.99 as it stands; about £9.99 if it were cut and finished.

At the endno · 3/5

Not in this form: the central promise has placeholders, the hands-on part repeats the argument, and I have the best of it from the free first edition and the chapters I read. A real person's honest thinking is in it and the middle chapters are good, but it is long, repeats itself, promises a company it does not tell you how to form, and keeps coming back to the author's products.

The interview

1. In one sentence, what is this book?
A book for founders in the age of coding agents, in the author's voice, with the product-selling, process and tutorial material of a newsroom wrapped round a genuinely good middle on funding, shipping and agent control.
2. Who is it for? Is it for you, and which part?
For the Gen Z graduate and for me, a Gen X expert. The parts for me are ch 17 on expert founders, ch 21 on funding, ch 24 and 25 on keys and agent control, and ch 27 on shipped. The job-hunting part (ch 10 to 12) and the storyboard and newsroom material (ch 34, 36, 41) are for someone else.
3. Did you believe it? What made you believe it, or not?
Mostly, because of the self-correction. Ch 16 admits 30 customers was ten and nine months was eight. Ch 24 corrects his own GDPR claim. Ch 21 lays out the evidence against his own thesis. What stopped me believing it fully: three of the chapters I liked are about his own products, and the ch 17 founder examples are people he helps.
4. Where did it lose you, or make you guess?
Ch 2 lost me, it is mood. Ch 24 lost me in the key types. Ch 13 and ch 30 are the same chapter, and I stopped trusting the structure when I saw that. Ch 22 and 37 are placeholders for the very thing the title promises, forming a company.
5. The pacing: where did it drag, and where did it rush?
It dragged in ch 2, 5 and 41, and in the hands-on part where ch 32, 33 and 39 repeat arguments already made in ch 7 and 19. It rushed the company itself, ch 22 and 37, where the promise is greatest and the text is smallest.
6. The structure: does the order of the parts make sense? What would you move, cut or merge?
Merge 13 and 30, 19 and 39, 12 and 36, 7 and 32 and 33. Cut 34 from a founder book. Move ch 41 out to the website, keep one page. The hands-on part would be better folded into the chapter each exercise belongs to than set as an eleven chapter appendix that repeats them.
7. What is strongest? What would you underline, or send to somebody?
Ch 21 on funding by selling shares and lending back is the idea I would send round. The reach, mandate and gap model in ch 25 I would use tomorrow. The idle-cost test for serverless in ch 23 is a good one-liner. The Nokia inertia argument in ch 17.
8. What is weakest, or does not make sense?
The weakest are the placeholders (22, 35, 37), the duplicated workflow (13 and 30), and the notes to Dinis in the manuscript. Also the strong form of 'by the end of the book you have started a company' with no chapter that does it.
9. Did it read as written by a person, or by an AI? What made you think so?
Parts of it read as a person: the tangents, the admission his books stall, the 2010 keynote that half came true. Parts read as an agent working from sources: the repeated 'In 2018 I wrote' openings, the habit of closing each chapter on a neat line, and the repetition of the same phrases ('Execution just got cheap', 'twenty years inside a problem'). He tells you it is co-written, which I respect, and it still reads about 60 percent person.
10. Will you buy it? At what price, in which format? If not, what would have to change?
No, not at £9.99 in this form. At that price I would expect an edited book. If it were cut to 50,000 words, the placeholders written, the two overlapping chapters merged and the product examples diversified, I would pay £9.99 for the ebook and read it on my tablet.
11. One change. What is it?
Write the company chapters (22 and 37) from real steps, and cut the hands-on part to the chapters that do not repeat the argument.
12. Would you give it to somebody else? Who?
Yes, two people: a Gen X friend who left a security vendor and is thinking about starting up, to read ch 21 and 25 only, and a former colleague who runs a small agent team, for ch 14 and 27. Not the whole book.

Rating: 3/5. A real person's honest thinking is in it and the middle chapters are good, but it is long, repeats itself, promises a company it does not tell you how to form, and keeps coming back to the author's products.

What they would change, most important first

  1. Write chapters 22 and 37 as real steps for at least the UK, or change the title and the promise in chapter 1 to match what the book delivers.
  2. Merge the duplicates: 13 into 30 (or the reverse), 19 into 39, 12 into 36, and 7 with 32 and 33, so the hands-on part shows the exercise and the argument chapter makes the claim once.
  3. Cut the book towards 50,000 words; the 2018 edition was liked for being short, and the repeated openings 'In 2018 I wrote' and repeated phrases make this one feel padded.
  4. Use at least one example for security, pricing and sales that is not the author's own product, or label the product chapters as case studies.
  5. Remove the For Dinis notes and the placeholder sections from the sold book and shrink ch 41 to a page; move the process to the website.
  6. Open the book with the funding and shipping argument (ch 17, 18, 21) and move the wonder chapter and the graphs chapter later.
  7. Show the evidence behind the headline figures (the 2.7 million founder study, the Stripe median, the sgit commit example) in an appendix I can check from my tablet.
