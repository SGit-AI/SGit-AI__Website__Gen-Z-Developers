Generation Z, 22. “Should I take the job or start the company, and does this book tell me how to actually do the second?”

Everybody in this study is invented. The readers, their names and every word attributed to them were written by a language model (Claude Sonnet, one agent per reader) reading the candidate's text. It is not reader research, and no sentence in it is evidence about a real person or a real market. What is evidence is the candidate's own text: every quote a reader cites is checked by the build, word for word, against the unit it cites, in the frozen file whose hash is recorded here.

Who they are

situation
Final-year computer science student in Manchester. Uses coding agents every day for coursework and a side project, a revision app with about 200 users from her course. Has two graduate job offers she is lukewarm about, and a friend asking her to start something together after graduation.
arrives with
Should I take the job or start the company, and does this book tell me how to actually do the second?
knows
Coding with agents, prompting, GitHub, deploying a web app, what vibe coding means online.
does not know
What forming a company involves, pricing, selling, funding without investors, who Dinis Cruz is, what Wardley maps are, what SG/Send, sgit or RiskMandate are.
decides on
Whether a friend she trusts says it is good, and whether the first chapter talks to her rather than at her.
leaves when
She is lectured, given history she did not ask for, or reads the same point twice. Hustle-culture tone also loses her.
patience
low: reads on her phone in ten-minute bursts on the bus; skims anything that does not earn the next screen
format
phone, in the browser or an ebook app
reads like
Skims headings, reads openings and endings of chapters, stops at lists and anything she could act on today.

Nearest in the storyboard cast: zara.

Chapter 1 speaks to her, but the five chapters of Before you start (lucky to be here, graphs, where do you land, connect the dots backwards) read as the author's history and philosophy before she gets anything to do, and she starts skimming in part 1.
expected path
about (skim) → ch:1 (read) → ch:2 (skim) → ch:3 (skim) → ch:4 (skim) → jumps to Start the company or Hands on → ch:18 or ch:40
expected put down

expected buy

The interview

1. In one sentence, what is this book?
It is a book that wants me to start a GenAI company by the last page, and so far it tells me why, how to price and sell, and how to build with no server, but not how to form the company.
2. Who is it for? Is it for you, and which part?
It is for people starting a company with agents, and the author says Gen Z and Gen X, but the examples are mostly experts in their forties and big company buyers. I am the Gen Z reader he wants, but only parts 6, 7 and 9, and chapters 18, 19 and 40, were for me.
3. Did you believe it? What made you believe it, or not?
Partly. The honesty helps: the notes saying prices are hypotheses (ch 20), the GDPR correction (ch 24) and placeholders that say they are placeholders (ch 22, 37). What hurts is that a lot of the proof is the author's own companies, RiskMandate and SG/Send, which I cannot check.
4. Where did it lose you, or make you guess?
Chapter 1 and 2 with the 2018 history, chapter 3 with graphs, and chapter 4 with Wardley maps, because I did not see why I needed them to decide job or company. Chapter 17's code review company lost me too. RiskMandate was never defined where I met it in 19.
5. The pacing: where did it drag, and where did it rush?
It drags in part 1 (ch 1 to 5) and in the middle chapters that retell the 2018 book first. It rushes where it matters most: ch 22 and 37 on forming a company are a few hundred words and say they are empty. Chapter 39 repeats 19, so it also drags there.
6. The structure: does the order of the parts make sense? What would you move, cut or merge?
I would move part 9 (the hands-on chapters) to just after part 1, or interleave each hands-on chapter with the part it belongs to. I would merge 19 and 39, and 22 and 37, cut chapter 5 down to the pivot story, and move 8 and 28 to an appendix. I would put the job-or-company question in chapter 1.
7. What is strongest? What would you underline, or send to somebody?
The test in chapter 18 and 19, give it away then take it away and see if they miss it, plus the Stripe fee arithmetic (below about £5 the fixed fee is the price). I would send those two to my course friend. Chapter 38's credits table too.
8. What is weakest, or does not make sense?
The company chapters are empty, which is the book's whole promise. The history and the author's own products take up a lot of room. Chapter 29 on Portugal and chapter 34 on storyboards are about the book, not me.
9. Did it read as written by a person, or by an AI? What made you think so?
A person, mostly. It is in the first person, tells stories with dates and has odd bits like the XZ aside and admitting stalled Leanpub books. But the notes to Dinis, the heavy citation of numbers and the repeated chapters feel like an agent assembled it.
10. Will you buy it? At what price, in which format? If not, what would have to change?
No, not at this version. If the company chapters were real I would pay £9.99 for the ebook, maybe, and read the hands-on part on my phone. Paperback at £19.99 only if a friend says it works. It would have to say how to form a company and drop the repeats.
11. One change. What is it?
Fill the company-forming chapters, and open with the job-or-company question and a first afternoon of action instead of 2018.
12. Would you give it to somebody else? Who?
Maybe a course friend who is also asking about starting something, and my mum, who has been inside her field for twenty years, for part 6 and chapter 17.

Rating: 2/5. A promise I want, but the chapters that keep it are empty, and too much of what I read was history, the author's companies and repeats.

What they found

  1. loses the sale Ch. 37: Form your company, step 9
    The chapter that should make the reader a registered company says it is not written, and chapter 22 says the same. The book's objective, to start a company by the last page, cannot be met in this candidate. This is the reason I would not buy now.
    By the end of this chapter you will have a company: registered, with a name, a country and a bank account. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Placeholder confirmed: ch:22 says "The material is not written yet" and ch:37 says "in this candidate it is not written yet"; both hand over to notes for Dinis (the UK, Lithuania, Portugal and EU memos). A known gap, waiting on Dinis's memos; what the study adds is that it is the gap every reader names.

  2. loses the reader Ch. 39: Your first price and your first ten customers, step 10
    The hands-on pricing chapter repeats chapter 19 almost sentence for sentence, down to the same RiskMandate ladder, the same free-is-not-free argument and the same gym study. A reader who read 19 meets it twice and stops.
    Neither answer is a marketing problem. If friendlies will not take it free, a bigger audience will not take it either. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Measured: 8-word runs shared between chapters, as a share of the smaller chapter: ch:13 and ch:30 319 (29%), ch:19 and ch:39 169 (15%), ch:7 and ch:32 113 (11%), ch:7 and ch:33 107 (10%), ch:16 and ch:35 56 (7%), ch:12 and ch:36 48 (4%). The hands-on part restates the argument chapters it follows.

  3. loses the reader Ch. 1: Gen ZX: who this book is for, and what it is for, step 3
    The first chapter spends its length on 2018, the two generations, a founder-age study and the name, before saying plainly what the reader will do. A reader on a phone gets the promise in the fourth section.
    Eight years later they are entering the workforce for real, and a lot of them are not looking for a job. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Ch:1's sections are Two generations, Generation ZX, What it is for and The measure: the objective is in the third section, the company list in its fourth paragraph.

  4. costs trust Ch. 20: Sell, step 7
    Prices, programmes and examples keep being the author's own companies, and the chapter admits there is no paid run yet and the prices are hypotheses. It reads as selling the author's products, not as advice a student can act on.
    The plan is honest about where it stands: no paid run yet, and the prices are hypotheses to test with the first ten customers. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: partly Partly. Counted: sgit 38 mentions in 16 units, RiskMandate 26 in 15, SG/Send 12 in 5, "the newsroom" 112 in 36. RiskMandate is the worked example in ch:19, 20, 25 and 39 (three mentions each), sgit dominates ch:24 (12). There are outside examples too (Stripe's fee, the 2.7 million founder study, Nokia), so "always" is too strong; "the main worked example is usually the author's own" holds.

  5. costs trust Ch. 17: Be a founder: the expert founders, step 5
    The central company example is a code review business built on graphs and the author's own tools, for a reader who has just asked job or company. It is hard to tell whether it is advice or a pitch, and it is too big for a first company.
    What the company sells is a review attached to a repository that keeps the layered graph current per commit and shows each change at every altitude. ✓ verbatim
  6. worth fixing Ch. 30: An agentic workflow from zero, step 42
    The actionable hands-on workflows with a promised result are in the second to last part, 30 to 40. A reader who wants to act today should meet that style first, not after 29 chapters of argument.
    By the end of this chapter you will have a folder for every person you deal with, an agent that reads those folders and drafts for you, and six lines of rules that make it safe. ✓ verbatim
  7. worth fixing Ch. 4: Where do you land?, step 12
    Wardley maps, explorer, villager and town planner are taught before the reader has a reason to care, and the chapter tells you to find your centre of gravity first. It is the book's frame, but it is jargon for a reader who came with a job question.
    Every dot is a claim. The positions are judgements, and the judgements are the argument. ✓ verbatim
  8. minor Ch. 2: You are lucky to be here, step 4
    Telling the reader to read on paper with a pen sits oddly in a book people may read on a phone, and the chapter spends many words on history.
    Read it on paper, with a pen. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Ch:2's How to read this book opens "Read it on paper, with a pen."

What they would change, most important first

  1. Write the company chapters (22 and 37) before the book is sold: UK steps, costs, a bank account, in plain steps, with a student example.
  2. Merge 19 and 39 and the two company chapters so no reader meets the same point twice.
  3. Open chapter 1 by answering job or company, in the reader's voice, and cut the 2018 history and the generations section to a page.
  4. Move the hands-on chapters (30 to 40) up, next to the argument chapters they belong to, or put a hands-on route up front.
  5. Define RiskMandate, SG/Send and sgit when first met, and use more examples from outside the author's companies.
  6. Cut the Wardley and graph chapters (3, 4) down or move them after part 6, with the jargon explained in one plain line.
  7. Move the notes to Dinis out of the sold text, and give a student-specific credits column in chapter 38.
