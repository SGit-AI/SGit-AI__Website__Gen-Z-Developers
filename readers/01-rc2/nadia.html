Nadia Rahman (invented)

Generation X, 51. “At 51, with no coding, can I really build the first version of this myself, and what would it take to leave my job and run it as a company?”

Everybody in this study is invented. The readers, their names and every word attributed to them were written by a language model (Claude Sonnet, one agent per reader) reading the candidate's text. It is not reader research, and no sentence in it is evidence about a real person or a real market. What is evidence is the candidate's own text: every quote a reader cites is checked by the build, word for word, against the unit it cites, in the frozen file whose hash is recorded here.
Put it downCh. 37: Form your company

The book promised on its first page that by the end I would have started a company. The two chapters that say how to form one, 22 and 37, are placeholders, and the second has even less than the first. I had the method for testing an idea and the arguments, not the steps.

Buy, at that pointmaybe

The first half of the book, chapters 6 to 9 and 13, and the sell and ship chapters, were worth my pencil, and the author is honest about what he has not done. But the part I came for is a note saying he has not recorded it yet. I would not pay for a promise, and I would pay for the finished edition. Format: paperback. Would pay: £19.99 if the company chapters were written; not today.

At the endmaybe · 3/5

I would buy the paperback at £19.99 once the chapters on forming the company are written and the first fifty pages let me do something; as it stands, the useful half is outweighed by a long slow start and examples that are mostly the author's own. Chapters 6 to 9, 13 and 17 to 21 are worth reading, but the book promises I will start a company and the chapters that say how to form one are not written.

Who they are

situation
Twenty-two years in insurance claims operations in Leeds, now head of claims operations at a mid-sized insurer. Has described the same triage tool to three vendors and two internal IT projects; none was built. Has savings, a mortgage nearly paid, a daughter at university (Generation Z) who keeps telling her to just build it with AI. Has used ChatGPT, never an agent.
arrives with
At 51, with no coding, can I really build the first version of this myself, and what would it take to leave my job and run it as a company?
knows
Her domain deeply, buyers in insurance, procurement, regulation (FCA), budgets, managing teams, how vendors sell.
does not know
How agents work, what serverless means, how to form or fund a company, what explorer coding or a graph of ideas is.
decides on
Whether the author has done it and shows it, whether the steps are concrete, whether it respects her experience.
leaves when
It talks down to her generation, it is vague where she needs specifics, or it is more about the author than about her.
patience
high, but she hates waffle: reads every chapter she starts, and judges hard
format
paperback, with a pencil
reads like
Reads in order, underlines, writes questions in the margin, checks claims against what she knows of her own industry.

Nearest in the storyboard cast: marta.

What we expected, before the run

hypothesis
The frame lands: chapter 1 and chapter 17 (the expert founders) are written about her, and she is the reader most likely to buy. She will be frustrated by the placeholder chapter on the company itself (ch:22) and by the UK steps being missing, which is exactly what she came for.
expected path
about → ch:1 to ch:22 in order, slowly → ch:37 (form your company) → ch:38 → ch:39
expected put down
none in the natural phase, or at ch:22 when the company chapter is a placeholder
expected buy
yes, the paperback, if the company chapters are filled; maybe as it stands

The interview

1. In one sentence, what is this book?
It is a book that tells an expert, in the author's own voice, that she can now build the first version of her company with agents, and mixes that with the story of how the author built and failed at his own products.
2. Who is it for? Is it for you, and which part?
It is for me, a Generation X subject expert, and for my daughter. Chapter 1 describes me nearly exactly. The parts that are for me are 6 to 9, 13, and 17 to 21. Chapters 3 to 5, 10 to 12 and 23 to 27 are for someone else, a developer or a graduate.
3. Did you believe it? What made you believe it, or not?
Mostly yes, because he shows his failures: the thirty customers corrected to ten in chapter 18, the list of what the agent corrected in chapter 6, the unfinished chapters. I did not believe the opening of chapter 2, which rests on an essay he could not read, and I did not believe the code-review company at the end of chapter 17 had anything to do with me.
4. Where did it lose you, or make you guess?
In chapter 3, with the code section, in chapter 4, with the eight-row table, and in chapter 5, which I read as the author thinking aloud. I guessed what the harness is in chapter 7, and what a Durable Object is in chapter 40. And I was lost when chapter 17 turned into a business plan for a code-review company.
5. The pacing: where did it drag, and where did it rush?
It dragged in chapters 2 to 5, about 8,000 words of wonder, graphs and maps before I built anything, and again in chapter 21 on funding. It rushed in chapter 22, which is a placeholder, and at the end in 37, 38 and 40, where vocabulary arrives with no time to define it.
6. The structure: does the order of the parts make sense? What would you move, cut or merge?
I would move chapter 13 or the workspace afternoon up, to just after chapter 1 or 8, so a reader can do something in the first fifty pages. I would merge 13 and 30, 7 and 33, and cut or move 10 to 12 to a separate job-seeker book. Chapters 3 to 5 could be one. The hands-on part should not be at the end.
7. What is strongest? What would you underline, or send to somebody?
Chapter 8 on the briefing-only developer, chapter 9 on the prototype as a brief that is not yet a product, chapter 18 on the miss-it test, and chapter 20, the one who signs: dry, fair. I would send chapter 9 to my CIO and chapter 15's one-way and two-way doors to my risk committee.
8. What is weakest, or does not make sense?
The weakest are chapters 22 and 37, which are the promise, and the proportion of examples that are the author's own product, RiskMandate, SG/Send and sgit. Also the notes to Dinis that are left in the page. It sometimes reads as the author's portfolio rather than my route.
9. Did it read as written by a person, or by an AI? What made you think so?
Mostly a person, with a sharp voice, quirks like padel and the iMac, and some honest failure. But it also read as machine-made in places: the long lists of quoted figures, the repeating structure of 'In 2018 I wrote' at the start of nearly every chapter, and the same paragraphs in chapters 13 and 30, 7 and 33. The book says it is co-written, which I respect.
10. Will you buy it? At what price, in which format? If not, what would have to change?
Not yet. At £19.99 for the paperback if chapters 22 and 37 are written, and the personal-risk question of leaving a job gets an answer. I would not pay £9.99 for the ebook of the current text. To change my mind: the steps to form a company in the UK, and what it costs a person with a mortgage to do this.
11. One change. What is it?
Write chapters 22 and 37, and put the finished workspace afternoon before chapter 3. If I could only change one thing it would be the company chapters.
12. Would you give it to somebody else? Who?
Yes, to two people. My daughter, for chapters 1, 2 and 8, because she keeps telling me to just build it. And my CIO, for chapter 9. I would not give it to my team as it is.

Rating: 3/5. Chapters 6 to 9, 13 and 17 to 21 are worth reading, but the book promises I will start a company and the chapters that say how to form one are not written.

What they found

  1. loses the sale Ch. 22: The company itself: the UK, Portugal, Lithuania, the European Union, step 29
    The book's stated objective is that the reader has started a company, yet the two chapters that say how to form one (22 and 37) are placeholders with two lines on Portugal and Lithuania and nothing on the UK. A reader who came for this does not buy, or puts it down here.
    The book will say how when I have said how. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Placeholder confirmed: ch:22 says "The material is not written yet" and ch:37 says "in this candidate it is not written yet"; both hand over to notes for Dinis (the UK, Lithuania, Portugal and EU memos). A known gap, waiting on Dinis's memos; what the study adds is that it is the gap every reader names.

  2. loses the sale Ch. 17: Be a founder: the expert founders, step 24
    A 51-year-old with a salary, a mortgage and savings needs to know what leaving a job costs and how long she can last. Nothing in the book addresses personal runway, giving up a salary, or the regulatory duties of a business that sells to insurers. The access argument says starting is cheap but not how a person survives while doing it.
    But the direction is clear: the millions that used to be the price of starting are no longer the price of starting. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Searched the whole candidate: no mention of runway, salary, mortgage or savings, and nothing on leaving a job to found. The expert founder the frame is written for is the reader who would have to.

  3. loses the reader Ch. 4: Where do you land?, step 6
    Chapters 2 to 5 are about 8,500 words of wonder, graphs, Wardley maps and learning before the reader does anything. A reader who wants the first version of her tool is a long way from it, and the practical afternoon is in chapter 13 or later.

What they would change, most important first

  1. Write chapters 22 and 37: the real steps to a UK limited company, the bank account, what it costs, and what you would do differently. Without them the objective on page one is not met.
  2. Add a chapter or section for the person leaving a salary: how long to keep it, what savings to put in, and the first three months. Use the expert founders, the surgeon and the transcriber, as the case, with what they spent.
  3. Move the practical workspace afternoon forward, to just after chapter 8, and shrink chapters 2 to 5 into one or two so the reader does something within fifty pages.
  4. Merge the duplicated pairs, 13 and 30, 7 and 33, and cut chapters 10 to 12, which are for the job seeker, into a short companion section or another book.
  5. Define every technical term at first use (harness, Worker, R2, Durable Object, serverless) or move the infrastructure chapters to a later part for readers who want them.
  6. Add examples that are not the author's: one worked example for each of the pricing, selling and shipping chapters in an industry such as insurance, healthcare or law.
  7. Remove the notes to Dinis, the process chapter 41 and the provenance scaffolding from the sold book, and keep them on the website.
