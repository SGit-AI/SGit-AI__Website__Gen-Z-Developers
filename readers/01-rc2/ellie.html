Generation Z, 26. “Can someone like me, who has never coded, actually build and sell this, and will this book show me how without making me feel stupid?”

Everybody in this study is invented. The readers, their names and every word attributed to them were written by a language model (Claude Sonnet, one agent per reader) reading the candidate's text. It is not reader research, and no sentence in it is evidence about a real person or a real market. What is evidence is the candidate's own text: every quote a reader cites is checked by the build, word for word, against the unit it cites, in the frozen file whose hash is recorded here.
Put it downCh. 40: Ship a serverless product in a weekend

The weekend chapter, where I wanted help most, was a wall of terms I did not know, after three other chapters (3, 6, 7) that already tested my patience. I would not read a second hard chapter in a row, and it turned out to be about the author's own tools.

Buy, at that pointmaybe

Chapters 4, 8 and 13 spoke to me and I would pass them to my sister, but the chapter I wanted most, the weekend one, lost me, and the repetition and the author's products made me wary. Format: ebook on my Kindle. Would pay: £9.99 at most, and only on a sample.

At the endmaybe · 3/5

I would buy the ebook at £9.99 if the practical chapters came first, if chapter 40 worked for a non-coder and if the company chapters were written; as it stands I would wait for the reviews. A warm, honest book with a few chapters that are just right for me, held back by jargon, repetition, placeholders and the author's own products as the examples.

Account manager at a small marketing agency in Bristol. Has never written code. Uses ChatGPT for drafts at work. Has an idea she has been sitting on for a year: a booking and reminders tool for independent dog groomers, because her sister is one and runs the business from a paper diary and WhatsApp.

The interview

1. In one sentence, what is this book?
It is a book about how people who can describe what they want, not only people who code, can start a company with AI agents, written by someone who has built a lot of it himself.
2. Who is it for? Is it for you, and which part?
It says it is for Gen Z and for their parents who know a field well. I am the first of those and I was glad to see non-coders named. The parts that were for me were chapters 4 (where I land, I think I am a villager), 8 (anyone who can brief an agent is a developer) and 13 (the Contacts folder). The back half is for someone who already builds.
3. Did you believe it? What made you believe it, or not?
Mostly yes, which surprised me. I believed the spreadsheet stories in chapter 8 and the honest notes like 'I cannot now say who she was, so I will not put a name to it' in chapter 2. I believed less when the proof was always the author's own tools, like RiskMandate in chapters 19 and 39 and sgit in chapter 24, with no customers like me.
4. Where did it lose you, or make you guess?
Chapter 3 (code as a graph, blast radius, sgit commands) made me think the book assumed I code. Chapter 6 lost me in the corrections list. In chapter 19 I had to guess what 'an Agent Behaviour Policy as an encrypted vault' actually is. Chapter 40 lost me for good with Worker, R2 bucket and Durable Object.
5. The pacing: where did it drag, and where did it rush?
It dragged in chapters 2, 5 and 9, which say the same hopeful thing several times, and again where chapters 13, 30, 19 and 39 repeat each other. It rushed through chapters 6 and 23, which are lists of terms and products, and chapter 40 is a rush of unexplained names.
6. The structure: does the order of the parts make sense? What would you move, cut or merge?
Part 9 should come much earlier or be threaded in next to the idea it practises, because the practical chapters (13, 30, 39) are where I woke up. I would cut the duplicated chapter 30 or 13, merge 19 and 39, move chapter 41 to the end as an afterword, and shrink the cloud and security chapters (23 to 25) to what a non-coder needs. The three placeholders (22, 37, 38) cannot be sold as chapters.
7. What is strongest? What would you underline, or send to somebody?
Chapter 8: 'The language you already speak is the one you program in.' I would send chapter 8 and chapter 13 to my sister. I would underline the shipping definition in chapter 18: 'Not to plan, not to pitch, not to architect. Ship.' And chapter 19's card-fee arithmetic is true and useful.
8. What is weakest, or does not make sense?
Chapter 40, the weekend chapter, which should be the proof and is a developer's recipe with stories of a failed product. Chapters 22 and 37 are empty on the thing the cover promises, a company. And the book keeps using the author's products as the examples, not a normal small business.
9. Did it read as written by a person, or by an AI? What made you think so?
Mostly a person. It has odd, specific, human asides (the Spectrum, the blown-up phone line, the paywalled essay) and admits what it does not know. The AI side shows in the repeated structures, near-copied chapters (13 and 30, 19 and 39), the 'For Dinis' notes and the dense lists of facts with dates.
10. Will you buy it? At what price, in which format? If not, what would have to change?
As it stands, maybe. At £9.99 on my Kindle, not £19.99 for the paperback. I would buy it if the practical chapters came first and I could see one non-coder example from start to finish, and not before the company chapters are written.
11. One change. What is it?
Put a plain-language start-to-finish example early: someone who has never coded, with an idea like mine, going from idea to a first customer, in a weekend.
12. Would you give it to somebody else? Who?
Yes, chapters 8 and 13 as a short extract, to my sister and to a colleague who also has an idea. I would not give her the whole book yet.

Rating: 3/5. A warm, honest book with a few chapters that are just right for me, held back by jargon, repetition, placeholders and the author's own products as the examples.

What they found

  1. loses the sale Ch. 37: Form your company, step 49
    The book promises a registered company by the end, and the chapters that should deliver it (22 and 37) are placeholders. A buyer who came for that would not buy this version.
    By the end of this chapter you will have a company: registered, with a name, a country and a bank account. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Placeholder confirmed: ch:22 says "The material is not written yet" and ch:37 says "in this candidate it is not written yet"; both hand over to notes for Dinis (the UK, Lithuania, Portugal and EU memos). A known gap, waiting on Dinis's memos; what the study adds is that it is the gap every reader names.

  2. loses the reader Ch. 40: Ship a serverless product in a weekend, step 17
    The weekend chapter, the one a beginner turns to for something to do, is written for developers and piles up unexplained terms. It is where a non-coder would put the book down.
    one Worker, one R2 bucket and one Durable Object per vault, on the free plan ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Ch:40's core step is "a vault server of 83 lines, one Worker, one R2 bucket and one Durable Object per vault"; none of the four terms is defined in the chapter.

  3. loses the reader Ch. 3: Everything is connected: graphs, step 5
    The graphs chapter starts well with the detective board and then moves into code review and the author's command line tool, which tells a non-coder the book assumes she codes.
    one commit of 75 lines added and 7 removed in three files turned out to reach nine of the tool's 72 commands and six of its eleven user stories ✓ verbatim
  4. costs trust Ch. 19: Price it, then give it away, step 14
    The proof is nearly always the author's own products (RiskMandate, sgit, SG/Send), with no example from an ordinary small business. It reads as a book about his companies and the pricing example is hard for a reader to transfer.
    For RiskMandate the thing is an Agent Behaviour Policy for one agent, as an encrypted vault the customer holds the keys to ✓ verbatim

    Checked: partly Partly. Counted: sgit 38 mentions in 16 units, RiskMandate 26 in 15, SG/Send 12 in 5, "the newsroom" 112 in 36. RiskMandate is the worked example in ch:19, 20, 25 and 39 (three mentions each), sgit dominates ch:24 (12). There are outside examples too (Stripe's fee, the 2.7 million founder study, Nokia), so "always" is too strong; "the main worked example is usually the author's own" holds.

  5. worth fixing Ch. 30: An agentic workflow from zero, step 15
    The Contacts folder workflow, the six rules and the five exercises appear almost word for word in both ch 13 and ch 30, and the pricing argument in ch 19 returns in ch 39. The repetition makes a 75,000 word book feel padded.
    Every mistake adds a line to the policy. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Measured: 8-word runs shared between chapters, as a share of the smaller chapter: ch:13 and ch:30 319 (29%), ch:19 and ch:39 169 (15%), ch:7 and ch:32 113 (11%), ch:7 and ch:33 107 (10%), ch:16 and ch:35 56 (7%), ch:12 and ch:36 48 (4%). The hands-on part restates the argument chapters it follows.

  6. worth fixing Ch. 1: Gen ZX: who this book is for, and what it is for, step 3
    Notes to the author and open decisions sit inside the reader's text, and the 'about' unit describes the review process before the book starts. A buyer should not meet them.
    or a new book that grew out of it? Decision 014 leaves it open. ✓ verbatim

    Checked: holds Holds for the candidate: 47 notes marked For Dinis in 36 of 52 units. Expected in a release candidate (decision 009) and not in a sold book; the readers say so themselves. What it adds: the notes interrupt the reading for every reader, so a reader's edition without them is worth having before the next study.

  7. worth fixing Ch. 5: Connect the dots backwards, step 7
    Chapters 2 and 5 spend their length on wonder, optimism and learning theory before any practical step; the reader reaches something to do only in chapter 8 or 13.
    The reality is that we do a horrible job of teaching maths. ✓ verbatim
  8. minor Ch. 6: What generative AI is, step 9
    The 'Where I was corrected' list is a good honest habit but arrives as a wall of technical terms for a reader who only wanted to know what generative AI is.
    Inside the model there are vectors, not a graph. ✓ verbatim

What they would change, most important first

  1. Put one plain start-to-finish example early, of a non-coder with an ordinary small-business idea, and use it through the book instead of the author's own products.
  2. Write chapters 22, 37 and 38 before selling, or take the promise of a registered company off the cover.
  3. Rewrite chapter 40 for a person who cannot code: no Worker, R2 or Durable Object, and a no-code path to a page a stranger can use.
  4. Bring the hands-on chapters forward or put each beside the idea it practises, and cut the duplication between ch 13 and 30 and between ch 19 and 39.
  5. Trim the graph, security and serverless chapters (3, 23, 24, 25) to what a non-coder needs, and explain each term at first use (repository, fork, serverless, vault).
  6. Remove the 'For Dinis' notes and the process description from the reader's edition, and move chapter 41 and the about page to the back or the website.
  7. Shorten chapters 2 and 5 so that the first practical step arrives by chapter 4.
