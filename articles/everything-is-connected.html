Everything is connected: a graph is a network of nodes and edges, not a diagram; a map is a graph with positions, provenance is a graph, the world is groups of groups, and you can start anywhere

By Dinis Cruz, co-written with this site’s journalist, gen-z.journalist, in Dinis Cruz’s voice; the editorial responsibility is Dinis Cruz’s.

Abstract. Graphs are one of the most fundamental ideas in this book, and one to introduce sooner rather than later. I have met people for whom a graph is just a diagram, so the book has to show what we mean: a network graph, nodes and the edges between them. The easiest way in is the whodunit, the detective's board with the connections from A to B. A map is a graph with positions; a Wardley map is a graph with context. Graphs nest: there are graphs of graphs, fractal graphs, and even nature is a gigantic graph, from systems down to molecules and photons, where groups of groups form natural abstraction layers, which is how we think. That is how I see the world: it is made of connections, which is why provenance is a graph, and why so many of the relationships in this book are graphs. And because a graph has no top and no bottom, you can start anywhere: it does not matter where you start, it matters that you start, and get movement. The 2018 book already had this as an outline, which says I had been thinking in graphs for years and that it is a very hard paradigm shift to make, and two of its published chapters, on the DOT language and on syntax trees, were graphs without saying so. Since then the newsroom and graphs.sgit.ai have written a lot about graphs, with figures and visualisations, and this article says what the book should bring from them. The storyboards can now draw graphs too, including the detective's board.

A graph is not a diagram

I have met people for whom a graph is just a diagram, a picture with boxes. So the book needs to show what we mean, and the visualisations are the way to do it. What we mean is a network graph: nodes, and the edges that connect them.

The first of the graphs books on graphs.sgit.ai, Fractal Semantic Graphs, says it in its second chapter: "In this book a graph is a network: nodes and the edges that connect them. Never a chart, never a figure." It separates three things people mean by the word: networks, for analysis (who is connected to whom); storage, graph databases, for speed; and semantics, for what something means. This book uses graphs in the first and third senses, and almost never needs the second.

The whodunit

The way I sometimes explain it is the whodunit, the detective story: the board on the wall with the photos, the notes and the red string between them. The question is always a connection, from A to B: who knew whom, who was where, what links the two. That board is a graph. And as follow the rabbit holes says, this generation are very good detectives already.

A map is a graph with positions

In a way, a map is a graph with positioning. In my talk on why Wardley maps changed how I think, the first two shifts were exactly that: "Its a graph with context", and "When a graph becomes a Map". A Wardley map is a graph where each node has a place on two axes, how visible it is and how evolved it is, which is what makes it a map and not only a graph. That is why where do you land and this article belong next to each other.

Graphs of graphs

It is very important to talk about fractal graphs, and graphs of graphs, and how everything connects. In a way, even nature is a gigantic graph: from the system, down to the molecules, down to the photons; everything is connected to everything else. And it is groups of groups that form natural abstraction layers, which is how we think.

The newsroom's introducing fractal semantic graphs puts the consequence in two sentences: "There is no top and no bottom. There is only the altitude you happen to be looking from, and how much definition you choose to load at it." And one line from the graphs books says why the edges matter more than the nodes: "A node connected to nothing is literally meaningless."

How I see the world

Fundamentally, that is how I view the world: it is all made of connections. This connects to this, which connects to that, which connects to that. That is why provenance is a graph: where a claim came from is a chain of edges back to its source. As the newsroom's journalism becomes intelligence puts it, the record should be "not a pile of articles, but a graph whose every edge has a date and a source". And it is why so many of the relationships in this book are graphs: the influences, the paths, the evidence, the people in a team.

The graphs book has a line for the reader who thinks this sounds exotic: "you may already think in graphs without ever having called it that." Tracing what an unfamiliar system connects to, trusting a claim because of where it came from, asking what else breaks if this fails: that is thinking in graphs.

Start anywhere

And for me the other thing about graphs is the ability to start anywhere. It is very important that you never feel overwhelmed. It does not matter where you start; it matters that you start. It does not matter, in a way, whether you are going straight in the right direction; it matters that you get movement. You start to learn, you start to think about these things, you start to connect the dots, and you make movement. So start from a place, and then start connecting.

A graph has no front page. Whatever node you start from, the edges take you to the rest, which is why connect the dots backwards can say that a path only makes sense afterwards.

The 2018 book was already there

The 2018 book had an outline called Graphs, never written, and its four bullets are this article in miniature: "Graphs are everywhere", "I've been thinking in graphs for years", "very hard paradigm shift to make", "requires an ability to think in nodes and edges". And two of its published chapters were graphs without saying so: Dot Language, about drawing the diagrams that software never has, and AST, which opens by calling an abstract syntax tree "a graph representation of source code".

The brief

For the editor. Read team/editor/role.md, this article and its storyboard first.

  1. Place the graphs chapter early, before or with Wardley maps, since a map is a graph with positions. Done when the chapter map orders the two and says why.
  2. Choose the figures from the list above, and the worked example: one newsroom article's graph, shown and read. Done when the chapter map names them.
  3. Place the 2018 material: the outline Graphs, and the chapters Dot Language and AST as examples inside this one. Done when the chapter map says where.
  4. Put Your first graph in the hands-on part. Done when the part lists it.
  5. Prepare interview questions on this: where graphs first entered my thinking (the influences register asks the same); a graph I drew that changed a decision; how to explain "start anywhere" to someone who feels lost. Done when the script is in your briefs/.

Questions for me, kept apart:

  • Graphs before Wardley maps, or the other way round?
  • Which of my graph presentations is the one the 2018 outline meant by "see my presentation on Graphs"?

What this does not claim

  • "Nature is a graph" is a way of seeing, not a claim in physics; the book should say so.
  • The three meanings of "graph" are the graphs book's classification. Others classify them differently.
  • The 90% figure is the newsroom's measurement on five articles. It is not a general law.
  • The figures listed are candidates. Which ones are in the book is the editor's choice.
  • The detective board is a teaching device. Real investigations are messier than red string.
